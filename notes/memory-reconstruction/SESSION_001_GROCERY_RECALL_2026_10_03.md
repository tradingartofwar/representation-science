# Session 001 — grocery-trip reconstruction

**Date:** 2026-10-03  
**Status:** OBSERVATION / exploratory probe  
**Ground-truth status:** incomplete; an external comparison is pending  
**Participant:** Vance  
**Scope:** conscious recall behavior during one guided conversation

## Purpose

The session deliberately moved away from increasingly subtle introspection and toward a recent real episode that Vance could actually remember.

The working question was not "how is memory stored in the brain?" It was narrower:

> What retrieval and reconstruction behavior becomes observable when a recent episode is recalled under progressively more specific cues?

No neurological mechanism is inferred from these observations.

## Earlier probe that motivated the shift

Before the grocery episode, several spatial prompts produced a repeated pattern:

- thinking of the front of the home first produced the front-door entry area;
- thinking of the kitchen first produced the counter seen on entering;
- thinking of the grocery store first produced the store entrance.

In each case, the first conscious image was tied to a familiar access or transition viewpoint rather than a neutral overview.

When prompted to "move around" the scene, Vance could do so easily, but that capability was prompt-evoked and should not be mistaken for what was spontaneously present in the initial recall.

This led to a methodological correction: stop asking for increasingly subtle introspective distinctions and instead follow a concrete remembered episode.

## Episode selected

The most recent physical grocery-store trip, the previous day, with Shirley.

The initial cue "last time you physically went inside a grocery store" produced:

- a visual image of the store entrance;
- identification of the episode as yesterday with Shirley.

## Retrieval sequence

### 1. Entry and cart decision

The episode unfolded forward from the entrance.

Vance recalled thinking that a basket was unnecessary because only one or two items were needed. He then noticed that Shirley had a somewhat heavy bag and two phones that she could place in a rolling basket/cart, so he returned his handbasket and joined her.

A correction occurred during retelling: the "we don't need a basket" content had been an internal thought, not spoken dialogue. This is a useful example of preserving event meaning while the mode of occurrence can be misreported during reconstruction.

### 2. Shirley says the trip will be fast

Vance recalled Shirley saying they would be fast. His own internal response was essentially that he might as well be patient because she enjoys looking around.

This recalled segment contained several layers:

- remembered external speech;
- remembered private thought;
- a stable model of Shirley's habits/preferences.

The person-model and episode content should not be treated as the same source.

### 3. Observing Shirley's state

Vance described watching Shirley look around for things she might want.

He also distinguished observation from interpretation:

- observed: Shirley looking around;
- interpreted: she was thinking about what she might want.

Vance further recalled an active understanding of Shirley's state: she was getting away from work, spending time with him, and shopping, all of which he understood as enjoyable for her.

The trip's active goal therefore shifted from "quickly get my items" toward "let Shirley enjoy the outing and have some wanted time."

This is not a specific motor action but a remembered change in the active goal of the episode.

## Retrieval of purchased items

When first asked about the trip, the purchase list was not immediately available.

Vance said he knew he had gone there for something specific and could probably recover it by "fishing around until I connected with it."

A concrete image then appeared:

- his hand lifting a carton of small Coca-Colas by its handle.

He noted that this image could also be his general image for "getting a carton of Cokes," so the image itself should not automatically be treated as a literal replay of the exact episode.

### Context expansion from the Coke cue

When prompted to look around the Coke scene, another concrete event became available: Tally, the store inventory robot.

Vance recalled:

- the upright robot moving through the store;
- the robot saying words to the effect of "don't mind me, I'm checking inventory";
- the robot moving in front of another shopper's cart and stopping him;
- the shopper becoming motionless;
- Vance and Shirley laughing a little;
- the shopper quickly continuing after the robot moved;
- other shoppers glancing at the robot.

Vance was uncertain whether this happened before or after getting the Cokes and uncertain about the exact aisle. He reasoned that Tally may have been around fast-moving products such as soda or chips because empty shelf spaces would make inventory checking useful.

This gave a clean distinction:

- specific event content was relatively vivid;
- exact spatial/temporal binding was weaker;
- inference filled around the missing linkage.

### Interpretation versus observation

The shopper's reaction provided another useful split.

Remembered observation:
- the man stopped;
- he was relatively expressionless;
- he quickly moved on once unblocked.

Interpretation:
- the situation was novel for him;
- he may have been thinking "oh my gosh";
- he may have been in a hurry or had something else on his mind.

Vance could identify these interpretations as interpretations rather than direct perceptual memory.

## Departure cue

When cued specifically with "leaving the grocery store," the first image was another transition point:

- pausing at the place where items were taken out of the shopping cart to carry to the truck.

This repeated the earlier access/transition-anchor pattern.

Vance noted a general habit of walking faster than Shirley and sometimes going ahead to the vehicle. He initially said he thought he had gone ahead this time, but this was recognized as partly reconstructed from habit rather than firm episodic recall.

## Back-seat episode: a stronger episodic anchor

A much more concrete memory appeared when Vance recalled placing the purchased items in the back seat.

The scene was vivid because there was a small practical problem to solve: arrange the items so they would not slide onto the floor while driving around corners.

From that cue, a larger sequence became available:

- the Coke carton;
- a small pint of bourbon positioned alongside;
- cookies Shirley had bought;
- placing the Coke carton in the firmest position;
- reaching across the back seat to place the cookies toward the console where Shirley could reach them;
- getting out and closing the door;
- getting into the driver's seat;
- repositioning the cookies again;
- Shirley saying she was hungry;
- Vance telling her she could have a cookie.

Vance described this not as a whole bundle appearing at once, but as being "in the scene in a sequence."

This suggests that once a sufficiently concrete anchor was active, the episode became available as a temporally unfolding reconstruction.

## Sparse anchors and plausible filler

When asked to move backward from the cookie comment, Vance initially produced:

- closing the car door — felt remembered;
- adjusting the seat cover — immediately recognized as imagined rather than remembered.

This is a useful example of a plausible completion appearing inside a real episode while the participant can still distinguish it from stronger episodic recall.

The sequence did not behave like a complete stored movie. Some anchor points were clear while intervening actions were absent or only plausibly reconstructible.

## Visual versus auditory reconstruction

For the cookie exchange, the scene was visually present.

The auditory component was different:

- Vance did not literally "hear" the remembered speech internally;
- he described something more like imagining that he heard it, with a heard-like sensation.

When prompted for qualities of Shirley's voice, Vance reported that the question seemed to access a different representation: stored characteristics of Shirley that could be used to regenerate her voice in another scene.

Working distinction:

> episode content may supply what happened or what was said, while a stable person-model supplies how that person would sound.

This is an introspective report, not evidence about neurological storage.

## Pre-verification purchase recall

By the end of the guided reconstruction, Vance recalled exactly three purchased items:

1. carton of small Coca-Colas;
2. small pint of bourbon;
3. cookies purchased by Shirley.

He estimated **90% probability** that these three items were the complete purchase.

This state should be treated as frozen pre-verification recall.

The original receipt is unavailable because receipts are routinely discarded.

Shui (American nickname: Shirley) was to be asked, without leading details, what she remembers the two of them buying. Her answer would be an independent second memory, not definitive ground truth.

### Independent second-memory check

Shui independently confirmed that the purchase contained **three items**.

This corroborates Vance's frozen 90%-confidence estimate that the purchase consisted of three items. It does **not** by itself establish the identity of all three items unless Shui independently names them, and it remains a second human recollection rather than documentary ground truth such as a receipt or transaction record.

## Provisional observations from this session

These are observations or hypotheses, not established mechanisms.

### O1 — transition/access anchors

Multiple place recalls began at an entry, exit, or habitual transition viewpoint.

Possible hypothesis: familiar transitions may provide strong access nodes for episodic reconstruction.

### O2 — context can expand a sparse cue

A concrete cue such as the Coke-carton action allowed a larger local episode neighborhood to become recoverable.

Possible hypothesis: retrieval may proceed by restoring relational context rather than by querying isolated facts.

### O3 — problem/novelty/consequence may strengthen recoverability

Two especially vivid episodes contained:
- novelty: encountering an autonomous inventory robot;
- practical consequence: arranging items so they would not slide in the vehicle.

Possible hypothesis: decisions, novelty, problems or consequences may increase later accessibility. This remains untested.

### O4 — event content and binding strength differ

Tally's event content was vivid, while exact aisle and temporal order were uncertain.

Possible hypothesis: an event can remain accessible while its exact spatial/temporal binding is weaker.

### O5 — interpretation can be richer than observation

Sparse observations of another person's behavior supported much richer reconstructed interpretations of their thoughts or state.

The participant was often able to distinguish the observed part from the inferred part.

### O6 — generic models can participate in episodic reconstruction

Stable models of:
- Shirley's habits and voice;
- routine walking behavior;
- generic object/action imagery;

appeared capable of supplementing episode-specific content.

This makes it important to distinguish "remembered from this episode" from "generated from what is generally known."

### O7 — plausible completion can appear inside recall

The imagined seat-cover adjustment demonstrates that a plausible action can emerge during reconstruction without carrying the same episodic status as stronger anchors.

### O8 — active goals may organize salience

Vance recalled that the shopping trip's purpose changed from a quick personal errand into supporting Shirley's enjoyment of time away from work.

A possible question for future testing is whether active values/goals influence which episode details later remain easiest to recover.

### O9 — confidence and completeness should be frozen before verification

The session ended with a 90% completeness estimate for a three-item purchase list.

Future sessions should preserve:
- spontaneous output;
- cue-assisted additions;
- confidence before verification;
- independent evidence afterward.

## Methodological lessons

1. Do not ask increasingly subtle introspective questions until the participant starts manufacturing distinctions to satisfy the prompt.
2. Prefer a real, recent episode with identifiable external traces.
3. Separate spontaneous recall, cue-assisted recall, inference, routine knowledge and imagined completion.
4. Record when a prompt itself changes the representation being inspected.
5. Ask for confidence before verification.
6. Treat a second person's recollection as independent evidence, not ground truth.
7. Prefer receipts, photos, messages, calendar records or other external traces when available.
8. Preserve failures and gaps instead of forcing continuous reconstruction.

## Relation to Representation Science

This session is relevant to the project's emerging distinction among:

- active representation;
- retained/navigable state;
- reconstruction;
- external recovery;
- required resolution under consequence.

A possible human-facing formulation is:

> the consciously active representation may be sparse, while a cue or demand can reconstruct a richer local scene from retained relational structure.

That remains a working hypothesis. The session does not show what physical information is stored, where it is stored, or how biological memory implements reconstruction.
